# Evidence

## The problem is real
What I need to show: students who spend on one or two habits every week (food runs, cash purchases) don't know their real total. For the person in problem.md, the estimate is somewhere between $260 and $600 a month ($3,100–7,200 a year), and the width of that range is part of the cost. Tracking only happens in their head, and there is no weekly limit.

### What research says

**How common it is: students who don't track spending**
- 43% of students surveyed said they don't track their monthly spending. LendEDU, 2016 College Students and Personal Finance Study, 455 students at three East Coast schools. *Agent opened the page and quoted it word for word. The study is old and small.*
  https://lendedu.com/blog/college-students-and-personal-finance-study

**How common it is: small, repeat treat spending**
- 62% of Americans buy themselves a small, affordable treat at least once a month. Among people who treat themselves, 52% spend $25 or less per purchase. CNBC/SurveyMonkey, 2026, 2,038 US adults. *Agent checked it from a page summary, not word for word. I need to confirm it.*
  https://www.surveymonkey.com/curiosity/treatonomics-consumer-spending/
- Gen Z averages 5.1 takeout meals a month, more than double baby boomers (2.5). FinanceBuzz, September 2026, 2,000 US adults, sponsored by Bank of America. *Agent quoted it word for word.*
  https://financebuzz.com/convenience-economy

**What it costs**
- 18% of millennials say they spend more than $200 a month on impulse purchases. Motley Fool Money survey, February 2026. The sample size isn't stated, and the survey is about millennials, not students. *Agent checked it from a page summary. I need to confirm it.*
  https://www.fool.com/money/credit-cards/articles/this-is-how-much-impulse-spending-is-costing-millennials-every-month

**Why card purchases are easy to overspend on**
- In an MIT study (Prelec & Simester, 2001), people told they would pay by credit card bid about twice as much for the same tickets as people paying cash. This is the "pain of paying" effect: card payments feel less painful, so people notice them less. *Only seen in search results. The agent did not open it. I need to open it before using it.*
  https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-821-listening-to-the-customer-fall-2002/5cc52570803b6e1bbea64585acdce779_redblack20copy.pdf

**Where good evidence wasn't found**
- **Underestimating spending.** I found no study showing that students *underestimate* what they spend on habits. That's the core claim in problem.md, and right now it rests on one person's own numbers.
- **Does tracking cut spending?** I found no solid study showing that tracking purchases reduces spending. That's the claim the app depends on.
- **Pizza or college-student food spending specifically:** no good numbers.
- **Unverified, so left out:**
  - Inceptia's "13% of students don't track" (the PDF text couldn't be read).
  - College Ave's "55% track their spending" (the page blocked the agent).

## The app helps
Who tests it: a classmate, on November 17.
What they do: read the web page, then do one task in the app: **log a pizza purchase and find out how many trips they have left this week.**
What I write down:
- How long the task takes, from opening the app to seeing "trips left." Target: under 30 seconds.
- Every place they get stuck, hesitate or ask a question.
- Whether they can explain, in their own words, what the weekly limit is and how it shrinks each month.
What would tell me I am wrong:
- Logging a purchase takes longer than 30 seconds, or they need help to finish it.
- They can't find or read the "trips left" number without being told where it is.
- After reading the web page, they can't say what the app is for.
